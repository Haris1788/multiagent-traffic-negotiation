01
Motivation · Why this problem

Intersections are where autonomy gets hard

A four-way intersection has no traffic light and no central controller — just several cars that each want to cross, right now, without crashing. It is the classic multi-agent negotiation problem.

  • Spatial reasoning is the bottleneck. Language models are strong with words, but reasoning about positions, directions and timing is exactly where they slip.
  • Instinct fails under pressure. "Just head for the goal" ignores who else is arriving at the same spot on the same turn.
  • Safety is all-or-nothing. One wrong step is a collision — there's no partial credit on the road.
The gap we target

A model answering on instinct will happily drive two cars into the same cell.

Real self-driving systems solve this with hand-built planners. We ask a sharper question: can the way we prompt the model alone turn it into a competent negotiator?

How the system works · The big picture

One intersection, one repeating loop

1
Set up the intersection

2–4 cars are placed on the grid, each with a starting point and a goal exit.

↓
2
Each car's driver decides a move

Given what it can see, every car picks its next step — go, speed up, turn, or wait.

↓
3
The world applies the moves & enforces the rules

Speed limits, no diagonals and braking distance — illegal moves are rejected.

↓
4
Check for collisions & arrivals

Two cars in one cell counts as a collision; a car on its goal has arrived.

↺ repeat every turn — until all cars exit or time runs out
✓
Score the run

Did everyone arrive safely? How many collisions? How many turns did it take?

The one thing we change

Two interchangeable drivers.

Everything else stays exactly the same — the same grid, rules and scoring. We only swap the "brain" that decides each move:

Zero-Shot
Answers on instinct — in a single shot.
ReAct
Reasons step-by-step before it moves.

Because only the driver changes, any difference in the score comes from the reasoning — nothing else.

03
The simulation · The world the cars live in

A grid intersection with real rules

  • A 10×10 grid forming a four-way intersection — no traffic light, no central controller.
  • 2–4 cars, each with a fixed start and a goal exit on the far side.
  • Every turn, all cars move at the same time, then the world checks what happened.
  • The world is exact and predictable — it never guesses, so every run is repeatable.
The rules of the road
  • Move 1 or 2 cells per turn — straight only, never diagonally.
  • A fast car can't stop dead — it must slow down first, like real braking.
  • Two cars, one cell = a collision — and it is counted against the run.
  • Illegal moves are simply rejected by the world.

Approach 1 · The baseline

Zero-Shot: decide on instinct

Baseline · our control
  • The car is shown the grid, its goal and the other cars — then asked directly for its next move.
  • One question, one answer — no explanation, no deliberation.
  • It answers the natural question: how well can a model drive on gut feeling alone?

Approach 2 · The comparison

ReAct: reason first, then move

Structured reasoning
  • Before choosing, the car thinks about the other cars and the risk of a crash.
  • It follows a simple pattern — a thought, an action, then an observation of the result.
  • Same task, same rules: the only change is that it reasons before it commits.
A single decision, out loud
Thought
The other car is heading down; if I speed up, we'd meet in the middle.
Action
Move one step — carefully — instead of two.
Observation
The path is clear now; the other car passes first. No conflict.

Under the hood · How a run is driven

Coordinating the cars, turn by turn

Built with LangGraph
  • Each turn, every active car is asked for its move, the moves are applied together, and the world is checked — then it repeats.
  • The loop runs until all cars arrive or time runs out.
  • The whole loop is organised as a LangGraph workflow — a state graph where each stage (decide → move → check) is an explicit, reliable step.

06
Summary · Conclusion · Limitations

Where the project stands

Summary

  • A realistic grid intersection with real movement rules and collisions.
  • Two interchangeable drivers — instinct-based and reasoning-based — behind one identical setup.
  • A live visual demo, plus a fair way to score and compare every run.

Conclusion

  • A clean, controlled comparison — only the reasoning strategy changes.
  • The design isolates the effect of reasoning from every other factor.
  • The final results will show whether reasoning genuinely beats instinct here.

Limitations

  • Toy environment. A small 10×10, turn-based grid leaves out continuous motion, real vehicle dynamics, sensors and pedestrians — so results shouldn't be read as claims about real-world driving.
  • Narrow, noisy evidence. Only 2–4 cars at a single intersection, driven by one free local model whose answers vary run to run; with a limited number of runs, the numbers are indicative rather than statistically strong.
  • A confounded comparison. Part of any measured gap can come from the safety guardrail or the exact prompt wording — not purely from reasoning.
